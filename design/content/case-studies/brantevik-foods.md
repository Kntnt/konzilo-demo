Brantevik Foods prepares ready meals and frozen vegetables for Nordic retailers at its plant in Tomelilla, and keeps them at −25 °C in cold stores there and at its distribution site in Kristianstad. Refrigeration takes most of its electricity, and it never stops.

## The problem

Two figures on Brantevik's network bills worried its operations director, Nils Thulin. The first was the peak. Tomelilla's highest demand came on summer afternoons and winter mornings, when compressors, ovens and the blast freezers all ran at once, and the peak set a growing share of the network charge. The second was growth: new lines had raised the company's electricity use by a fifth in five years. A larger connection at Tomelilla was possible, but it would have cost more every month, for capacity used a few hours a week.

## What we built

- 1.9 MWp of solar on the plant at Tomelilla, and 1.2 MWp on the cold store at Kristianstad
- A battery of 2 MW and 4 MWh at Tomelilla, in two containers on a concrete pad 14 metres from the cold store
- Ampfield Pilot, linked to the cold stores' own control system, so that it can lower their set point when the sun is up

Brantevik bought the systems outright, and we run them under a service agreement.

## Keeping the sun as cold

The cold stores hold thousands of tonnes of frozen food, and that mass is a store in itself. When the roof gives more than the plant uses, Pilot lowers the stores' set point from −25 °C to −28 °C. The air cools first and the goods follow slowly. In the late afternoon and evening the compressors can rest while the stores drift back towards −25 °C, and the battery covers what is left of the peak.

Brantevik's quality team set the limits. The product's temperature stays inside them, and if any store's sensors read outside the range, Pilot stops pre-cooling that store and hands it back to its own control.

## Working in a food plant

Food safety came before speed. The roof work at Tomelilla ran above open production, so:

- every roof hatch over a production area was sealed, and the crew reached the roof by a stair tower outside
- the installers took the plant's hygiene induction, and none entered a production area during the works
- the battery was connected to the main switchboard in a planned stop of six hours, at four on a Sunday morning, when production was down and the cold store doors stayed shut

During the stop, the cold store's air temperature rose by less than one degree.

## What it gave

The figures for the first year, May 2025 to April 2026, against the year before:

| At Tomelilla and Kristianstad | Before | First year |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Peak demand at Tomelilla, monthly average | 2.6 MW | 1.9 MW |
| Electricity from the roofs | None | 2.8 GWh |
| Share of the year's electricity from the roofs | None | 23% |
| Power exported to the grid | None | Under 2% of the output |

Almost every kilowatt-hour from the roofs is used on site, because the compressors are always running. The peak at Tomelilla fell by 27%, and the network charge with it.

> We used to plan production around the compressors. Now the compressors plan around the sun, and nobody on the floor has noticed anything except the bill.
>
> <cite>Nils Thulin, Operations Director, Brantevik Foods</cite>
