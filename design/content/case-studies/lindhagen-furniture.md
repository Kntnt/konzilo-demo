Lindhagen Furniture has made tables, chairs and storage in Värnamo since 1961, and a third generation of the family now runs it. The factory's roofs cover 26,000 m², and by 2023 its chief executive, Maria Lindhagen, had decided they should produce power. The question was how to pay for it.

## The problem

The factory uses about 5.9 GWh of electricity a year, for its machines, its dust extraction and the drying of lacquer. In the same two years the board had approved a new CNC machining line, and the company's capital was spoken for. A solar system bought outright would have paid for itself, but it would have competed with the line for the same money, and the line came first.

## The agreement

We offered a power purchase agreement. We built the system at our cost, and we own it for 15 years. Lindhagen buys every kilowatt-hour it produces at a price fixed for the whole term, set below what the factory paid for grid power when it signed. We insure, monitor and maintain the system, and we replace its inverters when they need it. When the term ends, Lindhagen can buy the system at the price the agreement sets, extend the agreement at a lower price, or have us remove it and restore the roof.

## Building over a working factory

A furniture factory has two hazards that a roof crew must respect: fine wood dust, and the solvent vapour of the lacquer shop.

- Every cable route keeps clear of the dust extraction ducts and the lacquer shop's zone. That meant 400 metres more cable, and no new risk.
- The inverters stand in a room of their own, behind fire-rated walls, with their own ventilation.
- The system rests on ballast, so the roof work needed no welding, burning or other hot work.
- Lifts were done at six in the morning, before the first deliveries of the day.

The factory did not stop for a day. The system was connected to the main switchboard during a planned stop of two hours on a Saturday.

## What it gave

In its first year, April 2025 to March 2026, the roof gave 1.91 GWh, or 868 kWh for each kWp. The factory used 1.83 GWh of it, 31% of its electricity. The rest, mostly on weekends in May and June, went to the grid.

Lindhagen paid nothing for the system and nothing for its maintenance. Its only cost was the power, at the agreed price, and in the first year that price was below what the same kilowatt-hours would have cost from the grid in every month.

The new machining line went into production in October 2025, on schedule, and paid for with the company's own capital.
