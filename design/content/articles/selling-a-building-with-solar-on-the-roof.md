Since 2019, 23 buildings with our systems on their roofs have been sold. In 21 of the sales the system passed to the new owner without delaying anything. In the other two, the buyer's surveyor asked for something the seller could not produce quickly: the structural calculation for the roof at one, and a year of measured output at the other, whose owner had cancelled its monitoring. Both sales went through, a month late. In Britain, where industrial and retail property changes hands often, I have learned to ask about the sale pack at the handover, not when the building goes to market.

## A system you bought

A system bought outright is part of the building, and goes with it unless the sale contract says otherwise. The panels, the mounting, the inverters and the cables are the buyer's on the day of completion. Several things around them do not move by themselves.

- **The service agreement** can be assigned to the buyer with our consent, which we give, or it ends at the sale.
- **The warranties** on the panels and inverters usually follow the equipment, but the makers want to know who owns it. Send them the buyer's details.
- **The export contract** with the electricity supplier ends with the seller's supply. The buyer needs one of its own from the first day, or the roof's surplus goes to the grid unpaid.
- **The grid connection** stays with the site, but the network operator must be told who now answers for the system connected to it.
- **The data** belongs to the seller. Pilot keeps ten years of it, and a buyer will want the history; agree in the sale that it moves.
- **The insurance** is the buyer's to arrange from completion.

## A system under a power purchase agreement

Under a power purchase agreement the system is ours, and the agreement follows the building. A buyer who occupies the building takes it over and goes on buying the roof's power at the agreed price. If the building is let and the tenant buys the power, nothing changes for the tenant. The new owner takes over the consent for the system to stand on its roof.

A buyer who does not want the agreement can end it, at the price the agreement sets for that year, which is also the price at which it may buy the system. We wrote about those clauses two weeks ago in [Who carries what in a power purchase agreement](/insights/who-carries-what-in-a-power-purchase-agreement/). In our experience buyers seldom end one. A known price for a share of the building's power, with someone else answering for the roof, is easier to keep than to replace.

## What moves, and what does not

| | Bought outright | Under a power purchase agreement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The system | Goes to the buyer with the building | Stays ours |
| Service and repairs | The agreement is assigned, or ends | Included, as before |
| Warranties | Follow the equipment; tell the makers | Ours to claim |
| Export contract | The buyer signs a new one | The buyer signs a new one |
| Grid connection | The operator is told of the new owner | The operator is told of the new owner |
| Measured data | Moves only if the sale says so | We report to whoever buys the power |
| Insurance | The buyer's | Ours |

## The sale pack

Keep these together from the handover, and a buyer's questions can be answered in a day.

1. The as-built drawings: the roof layout with walkways and fire breaks, and the single-line diagram
2. The roof survey and the structural calculation
3. The commissioning and test reports, and the network operator's permission to export
4. The warranties, with the equipment's serial numbers
5. The service agreement, with its record of inspections and faults
6. Three years of measured output, month by month, against the forecast in the offer
7. The power purchase agreement, if there is one, with its table of prices for ending it

## What a buyer will pay for it

A buyer's valuer counts a solar roof only as far as the figures prove it. Three years of measured output, a roof whose membrane has twenty years left, and inverters with ten years to go are worth something in the price. A roof with no measured history, or with inverters due for replacement next year, is worth less, and a careful buyer deducts the €50,000 to €60,000 per MWp that new inverters cost.

Buyers of industrial and retail property in Britain now ask for a building's energy figures as part of their checks. A roof with measured output answers many of those questions at once, and a roof without it raises more.

## If the buyer wants the panels gone

It happens rarely, usually when a buyer plans to rebuild. A system bought outright is then the seller's or the buyer's to remove, as the sale agrees, at a cost of lifting, disposal and making good the roof. Under a power purchase agreement, the agreement's table sets the price of ending it early, and we remove the system.
