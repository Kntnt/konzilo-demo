Our first roof, 180 kWp on a warehouse in Gothenburg's harbour, has produced power since October 2014. We still look after it, and the hundreds of roofs we have built since. What they have taught us is that a solar roof needs little, but needs it every time: someone who reads its data every day, a technician on it once a year, and a few large jobs that come round once or twice in its life.

## What stopped our roofs in 2025

Across every roof we run, faults and outages cost 1.6% of the output the panels could have given. This is where it went:[^1]

| Cause | Share of the output lost |
| --- | ---: |
| Inverters that stopped or tripped | 52% |
| Faults in strings: connectors, cables and fuses | 18% |
| Outages and limits on the grid | 12% |
| Storms, birds, and work by others on the roof | 9% |
| Dirt, moss and lichen on the panels | 6% |
| Everything else | 3% |

The panels themselves were almost never the problem. We replaced about one panel in 2,500, most of them cracked by hail or by a foot.

## Every day: someone reads the data

Ampfield Pilot reads every inverter once a minute and compares each string of panels with its neighbours. On a clear day, a string that gives 8% less than the one beside it has a fault, often a loose connector or a blown fuse. An inverter that stops on a June morning costs a 1 MWp roof up to 80 kWh an hour until someone notices.

Our operations centre in Gothenburg sees such faults within the hour and resets most inverter trips remotely. When a fault stops production and cannot be cleared from a screen, a technician is on site within two working days. Most of the output that maintenance saves, it saves here, not on the roof.

## Every year: the inspection

Once a year, in spring or early summer, a technician spends a day on a 1 MWp roof. The list is the same on every roof:

- the panels: cracked glass, burn marks, and frames that have shifted
- connectors and cables, with a thermal camera on a sunny day, to find the hot spot before it burns
- the mounting: ballast blocks in place, rails where they were laid, pads under them whole
- the membrane under and around the array: punctures, blisters and standing water
- the drains: leaves, moss and nests cleared
- the inverters: filters, fans and terminals
- the fire service isolation switch, tested, and its signs still legible
- walkways and edge protection

On coastal roofs, in Gothenburg, Malmö, Rotterdam and Teesside, gulls nest under the panels in spring and block the drains. A mesh fitted along the edges of the array keeps them out, and on those roofs we fit it at the build. After a storm with gusts above 25 metres a second, we inspect every roof in its path within a week.

## Every two or three years: the wash

Panels at 10° are washed less by rain than steeper ones. Dust, pollen and bird droppings build up, and on a roof near a road, a sawmill or a flour mill, faster. We wash when the data shows the loss is worth more than the wash, which on most roofs is every two to three years: with deionised water and soft brushes, never with detergent or a pressure washer, which can void the panel's warranty.

## Once: new inverters

Inverters last 12 to 15 years, and are replaced once in a roof's life. For a 1 MWp roof that costs €50,000 to €60,000 at today's prices, work included. If you own the system, put about €4,000 a year aside for it. Under a power purchase agreement it is ours to pay.

## Perhaps once: the membrane

A membrane lasts 25 to 30 years. If it was new when the panels went on, the two age together. If it had fifteen years left, the array must be lifted, the roof renewed and the array refitted, which on a 1 MWp roof costs around €80,000 on top of the roofing. That is why we survey the membrane before we build, and advise replacing it first if it has less than ten years left.

## What it costs

Our service agreement covers the daily monitoring, the yearly inspection, the washes, and the work on every fault, for €8 to €12 per kWp a year. It guarantees 98% availability and pays you for production lost below it. The new inverters and any lifting for the roof come on top.

Over thirty years, the upkeep of a 1 MWp roof comes to €290,000 to €420,000 at today's prices, inverters included. Spread over what the roof produces in that time, about 25 GWh, it is 1.2 to 1.7 cents a kilowatt-hour.

## If you do it yourself

Some customers with their own maintenance staff take on the inspection, and the handover of every system includes a day's training for them. What we ask them not to do: work on the DC side without training for it, pull a connector under load, walk on panels, or clear snow, which costs more in damaged panels and membranes than it gains in power.

The data is the part not to do without. A roof that nobody watches can lose a string for a summer, or an inverter for a month, and nobody notices until the year's figures come in.

[^1]: Ampfield's operations centre's log of faults and outages on every roof it ran in 2025, weighted by the output each one cost, as Pilot calculated it from the neighbouring strings and inverters.
