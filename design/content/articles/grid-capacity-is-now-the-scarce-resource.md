When Kessel Packaging asked its network operator for a larger connection to its plant in Dordrecht, the answer was three years. The plant needed more power for a new line and could not wait that long. It installed a 5 MWh battery instead, which discharges whenever the plant's demand nears its limit. The battery was running in five months, the plant has room for the new line inside its old connection, and its grid fees are 22% lower.

## Two pressures

Industry is moving heat, transport and processes from fuel to electricity, and the grid is being reinforced more slowly than demand grows. Operators are answering in two ways, and both reach your site.

The first is the queue. In congested parts of the Netherlands and England, a request for a new or larger connection can be met with a date years away. In Sweden and Denmark the waits are shorter, but they are growing around the larger cities and ports.

The second is the price. More of a network bill is now set by the highest demand in a month or a year, and less by the energy used. Across the 160 customer sites whose network invoices we follow, the share set by peak demand rose from about a quarter in 2021 to about two-fifths in 2025.[^1]

## What one peak costs

Take a factory that runs at about 1.5 MW but reaches 2.2 MW for twenty minutes on weekday mornings, when compressors, ovens and lights start together. If its operator charges for the month's highest peak, the factory pays for 2.2 MW all month. At an illustrative charge of €5 per kW per month, those twenty minutes cost €3,500 a month, or €42,000 a year.

Such a peak often comes from timing alone, since nobody chose to start everything at once.

## Three ways to lower it

1. **Stagger what can wait.** Start the compressors before the ovens, or let the chargers slow down in the first hour of a shift. It costs nothing, and it often takes a tenth off the peak.
2. **Store energy for the peak.** A battery charges in quiet hours and discharges when demand nears a limit you set. Brantevik Foods cut its peak demand by 27% this way, and its cold stores pre-cool on solar power so that the compressors can rest through the afternoon.
3. **Produce on site.** Solar lowers a peak only when the peak falls in daylight in the summer half of the year. Most industrial peaks fall on winter mornings, so solar seldom does this alone.

## What a battery earns

A battery seldom earns the most from peaks alone. It is worth most when it does several jobs: lowering the peak, moving power from cheap hours to dear ones, keeping midday solar for the evening, and, where the market allows, being paid to hold power ready for the grid. Ampfield Pilot plans these jobs a day ahead and gives your own peak first call, so that the battery is full when the morning comes.

For peak shaving alone, most of the batteries we built in 2024 and 2025 are paying back in six to nine years. With income from the grid, in five to seven. For Kessel, the battery's largest value shows on no invoice. It let a new line run years before a larger connection could have come.

## A connection that is not always firm

Several operators now offer connections that give full capacity most of the year but less at hours named in advance. For a site with a battery, or with chargers and loads that can wait, such terms can come years sooner than a firm connection. Ask what the reduced hours have been over the last year, not only what the contract would allow.

## Before next year's tariffs

Operators publish next year's tariffs in the last months of the year, and three things are worth doing before January:

- Read how your tariff measures the peak: per month or per year, over fifteen minutes or an hour, and whether a winter peak costs more.
- Find last year's peaks in your meter data, and what was running when they were set.
- If you plan to electrify, ask your operator now what capacity it can give, and when.

If the answers point at a battery, we can model what it would have saved in each of the last twelve months, from your own meter data, within two weeks.
