In 2024 Kessel Packaging wanted a new printing and die-cutting line at its plant in Dordrecht. The plant already ran close to the limit of its grid connection, and the network operator's answer to a request for more was a reinforcement costing €1.1 million, ready in three years. Kessel signed with our Rotterdam office in October 2024 for a 5 MWh battery instead. It was running in March 2025, the new line started in June, and the battery has now been through a full year, April 2025 to March 2026.

## The year in figures

| At the plant in Dordrecht | April 2024 to March 2025 | April 2025 to March 2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Electricity used | 15.1 GWh | 17.6 GWh |
| Highest demand from the grid, monthly average | 3.30 MW | 2.92 MW |
| Highest demand in the year | 3.52 MW | 3.14 MW |
| Capacity agreed with the network operator | 3.6 MW | 3.2 MW |
| Grid fees for capacity, peaks and energy, before taxes | €540,000 | €421,000 |

The plant used a sixth more electricity and drew less from the grid at its highest. Add the battery's discharge back into Pilot's minute-by-minute meter data, and the new line would have pushed the plant's demand to 4.1 MW on weekday mornings, half a megawatt more than the connection gave.

## How the battery did it

The battery has 2.5 MW of power in two containers beside the warehouse. Ampfield Pilot holds the plant's draw from the grid at 2.9 MW. When the morning shift starts the corrugators, the new line and the compressors together, the battery covers what goes over, usually for one to two hours. It recharges in the afternoon and at night, slowly enough that the recharging sets no peak of its own.

Over the year the battery ran about 190 full cycles, well within what its warranty allows. It was available 99.6% of the time; the one stop was a cooling fan in a container, replaced within a day.

In May 2025, once the battery had run for two months, Kessel lowered the capacity it had agreed with the network operator from 3.6 MW to 3.2 MW, and pays less for it every month. That is part of the fall in its fees. The margin was tested twice. On two mornings in January, after a cold night and a long Monday start-up, the battery reached the lowest charge Pilot allows it before the peak had passed, and the plant's draw rose to 3.14 MW for about twenty minutes, still inside the 3.2 MW.

## How to count what it is worth

The fees fell by €119,000. The battery cost Kessel €1.7 million. Counted on the fees alone, it pays back in about fourteen years, far slower than the six to nine years we expect of a battery bought for peak shaving.

That is the wrong sum for Kessel, whose alternative was €1.1 million for a larger connection and three years without the new line. Counted against the connection it replaced, the battery cost Kessel €600,000 more, and the fees repay that in about five years. The new line's three extra years of production show on no invoice of ours, and they were the reason for the project.

> We needed the new line in 2025, not in 2028. The battery was the only way to have it, and the lower bill came with it.
>
> <cite>Marieke de Wit, Plant Manager, Kessel Packaging</cite>

## What Kessel did not do

A battery that sits full on a Saturday could be paid to hold power ready for the grid, and we modelled what that would have earned. Kessel chose to keep the whole battery for its own peaks in the first year, while the new line settled in, and has not offered any of it to the grid. The line now runs two shifts, so the weekday hours the battery is needed have grown, not shrunk.

## For a site in the same position

Kessel's year shows three things worth checking at any site that has been offered a larger connection years away:

1. **The connection's real limit**, from a year of meter data, against the capacity in the agreement.
2. **How long the peaks last.** Kessel's peaks last one to two hours, which a 5 MWh battery can cover. Peaks of half a shift need a much larger battery, or a larger connection.
3. **The full cost of the alternative**: the reinforcement, the years of waiting, and what the plant would have lost in them.

If the third is larger than the price of a battery, the fees it saves are a bonus. Joost van der Heiden and the team in Rotterdam can model your site from a year of meter data, as they did Kessel's.
