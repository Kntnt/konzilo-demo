The question I hear most from fleet managers in England is whether the depot can take chargers at all. A request to the network operator for more capacity can come back with a date three or four years away and a bill for new cables and a substation. Before you make that request, check whether you need to.

## Do the sums in energy, not power

Take a depot with a 400 kVA connection, which gives about 360 kW in practice. The warehouse draws 150 to 250 kW in the day and about 60 kW at night. The fleet is 60 vans, each driving about 150 km a day.

If every van has an 11 kW charger and they all plug in at six in the evening, the chargers ask for 660 kW at once. Counted in power alone, the depot needs a connection almost twice the size it has.

Now count energy. A large electric van uses about 35 kWh to drive 150 km, so the fleet needs 2,100 kWh a night. The vans stand in the yard for ten hours or more, and 2,100 kWh spread over ten hours is 210 kW. Allow for the cold of a January night, when the vans use more, and it is about 250 kW. With the warehouse's 60 kW, the site draws around 310 kW, inside the 360 kW the connection gives.

## Let the chargers take turns

The gap between 660 kW and 250 kW is what load management is for. A controller follows the site's main meter and divides what the connection has spare among the chargers, answering within a second. When the warehouse's load rises, the chargers slow down; when it falls, they speed up. Vans that leave first are charged first. Drivers find a full van in the morning, and the connection never sees more than its limit.

Ampfield Pilot does this at every depot we build. At Norrvik Logistics' depot in Jönköping, 64 charge points for vans and trucks could draw about 2.4 MW if every vehicle plugged in at once. Pilot shares out what the distribution centre leaves spare, and the site has never gone over its limit.

## Where the sums stop working

Three things push a depot past what load management alone can solve.

- **Heavy trucks.** A 40-tonne electric truck on regional routes uses 300 to 400 kWh a day. Ten of them need 3,000 to 4,000 kWh a night, more than a small depot's connection can give in the hours they stand still.
- **Charging in the day.** A truck that tops up while it is loaded at noon needs 350 kW or more for that half hour, at the hour the warehouse is busiest.
- **Growth.** A connection that is enough for 60 vans may not be enough for 120.

For each there is something short of a new connection. A battery charges in quiet hours and carries the chargers through the busy ones; it is where a battery most often pays at a depot. Solar on the depot's roof covers part of the daytime top-ups from April to September. And changing when vehicles come back can do as much as either, at no cost.

## A connection that is not always full

In congested parts of England and the Netherlands, network operators now offer connections that give full capacity most of the year but can be cut back at hours they name in advance. A site that cannot wait years for a firm connection can often accept such terms, as long as its chargers and battery can be told to hold back at those hours. Ask your operator whether it offers one, and what the restricted hours have been over the last year, not only what the contract would allow.

## What to gather before you ask

1. A year of interval data from the main meter, half-hourly or finer
2. The capacity in your connection agreement, not the rating of the main fuse
3. Each vehicle's daily distance and the hours it stands in the yard
4. Your plans for the fleet over the next five years
5. The operator's indicative date and cost for a larger connection, if you have asked for one
