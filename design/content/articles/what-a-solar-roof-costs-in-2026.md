In 2022 the panels for a 1 MWp roof cost us about €270,000. This month the same capacity costs about €120,000. Customers who follow panel prices sometimes expect a whole system to have halved with them, and are surprised by our offers. A 1 MWp east–west system on a flat roof costs €620,000 to €720,000 installed at today's prices, about a fifth less than in 2022. The panels were a third of the price then. They are less than a fifth now.

## Where the money goes

This is how we priced a typical system this month: 1 MWp, east–west at 10°, ballasted on a membrane roof in southern Sweden, connected to the building's main switchboard.[^1]

The largest line is the people on the roof. After them come cable and switchgear, then the panels, which are no longer the first thing to look at in an offer.

## What changed since 2022

**Panels got cheaper and stronger.** A panel of the same size now gives about 435 W, where it gave 400 W in 2022. A megawatt takes 2,300 panels instead of 2,500, so it also needs fewer rails, less ballast and fewer hours of lifting.

**Work got dearer.** Wages for installers and electricians have risen in all four of our countries, and each roof takes more planning at height than it did. Installation has risen by about 15% per MWp since 2022, even with fewer panels to fit.

**The grid asks for more.** Network operators now ask for protection relays, studies and documents that a roof of this size did not need five years ago, and the work in the switch room has grown with them.

**Fire safety takes room.** Walkways and fire breaks now take 10 to 20% off the usable area of most roofs. A roof gives fewer kWp than it would have, while the cost of reaching it, fencing its edges and craning onto it stays the same.

When the panels were a third of the price, the offer with the cheapest panels often won. Now a panel 10% cheaper saves 2% of the system. An offer that leaves out the edge protection, or counts on a crane only on weekdays, can differ by more than that.

## What to check in any offer

Ask every installer you compare, us included, for these, each priced on its own line:

1. **The yield**, in kWh per kWp a year, the losses assumed and where the sunlight data comes from. Our model gives 870 kWh for an east–west roof in Gothenburg and 910 in Malmö. An offer that promises much more should say why.
2. **The roof.** Whether the structural check is included, and who pays if the membrane must be replaced before the panels go on.
3. **The fire layout.** Walkways, fire breaks and an isolation switch the fire service can reach from the ground, drawn on the roof plan.
4. **The grid.** Who makes the application, and what happens to the price if the operator asks for an export limit or a protection relay.
5. **Access.** Cranes, edge protection and lifts at the hours your site allows, not the hours that suit the installer.
6. **The inverters' replacement**, around year 12 to 15, and whether the service price covers it.
7. **Service after the first year**, per kWp and year. Ours is €8 to €12.
8. **The warranties**: whose they are, for how long, and who handles a claim when a panel fails in year nine.

Two offers that differ by €80,000 on the same roof seldom differ in the panels. Put them side by side line by line, and the gap usually turns out to be one of these eight.

## What the next year may bring

Panel prices have little left to fall, and makers have begun to raise them a little. Installation is the largest line, and it rises with wages. We expect system prices to move by a few percent a year rather than by halves, so a roof priced this spring should cost much the same next spring.
