More of our customers now report their emissions in detail, and the auditors who check those reports ask harder questions each year. On-site solar is one of the easier things to report, provided the meters and the contracts are in order before the year starts, not after it ends.

## Where solar shows up

Emissions from the electricity a company buys are its Scope 2 emissions, under the greenhouse gas accounting standard that almost every company reports by. Power you generate and use on site is not bought, so it adds nothing to Scope 2. A panel burns nothing, so it adds nothing to Scope 1 either. In practice, every kilowatt-hour from your roof that you use yourself is a kilowatt-hour less in Scope 2.

The standard asks for Scope 2 to be reported in two ways:

- **Location-based:** the power you buy, times the average emissions of the grid where you are.
- **Market-based:** the power you buy, times the emissions of what your contracts say you bought, such as a renewable tariff backed by certificates, or the residual mix where you have no such contract.

Solar you use yourself lowers both, because it lowers the amount you buy.

## What it is worth, in tonnes

How much depends on the grid you would otherwise draw from. The figures below are for a site that uses 4 GWh a year and gets 1.2 GWh of it from its own roof, with illustrative grid factors for each country.[^1]

| Country | Grid factor, kg CO<sub>2</sub>e per kWh | Scope 2 cut by solar, location-based |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sweden | 0.03 | 36 tonnes |
| Denmark | 0.12 | 144 tonnes |
| United Kingdom | 0.20 | 240 tonnes |
| Netherlands | 0.30 | 360 tonnes |

In Sweden, where the grid is already low in carbon, the location-based cut is small. Under the market-based method it can be several times larger, since the residual mix, the power left over once every certified renewable megawatt-hour has been claimed, carries far more carbon than the grid average in all four of our markets.

## Certificates: the part that goes wrong

Power from a registered generator can carry certificates that show where each megawatt-hour came from. A buyer who holds and cancels them may count that power as renewable.

If certificates are issued for the power you use on site and then sold to someone else, the buyer owns the claim. You may not count the same power as renewable under the market-based method, and must treat it as if you had bought it at the residual mix. Most rooftop systems are never registered, and the question never arises. Large ones are, and so are many systems under a power purchase agreement.

If you buy your roof's power under such an agreement, check that the certificates for it are transferred to you or cancelled on your behalf. Ours are, unless you ask otherwise.

## What to measure

1. Generation, from a meter at the inverters, hour by hour
2. Import and export, from the main meter, at the same resolution
3. Self-consumption: generation minus export
4. Whether certificates were issued for the power, and who cancelled them
5. Who owns the system, and under which contract

Ampfield Pilot records the first three for every site it runs and puts them in the monthly report, per site, by the third working day. Keep the contracts with the figures: an auditor will ask for both.

## Claims that will not hold

Exported power does not lower your emissions. It lowers someone else's, and the standard does not let you net it off against what you buy. You may mention it separately, as avoided emissions, but you may not subtract it.

A site that gets 31% of its electricity from its roof is not solar-powered, even on a sunny afternoon when it exports. Say what the share is.

Last, a reporting year is long enough for a system to break. A month's outage in June can cost 15% of the year's output, so report what the meters measured, never the forecast in the offer.

[^1]: The grid factors are rounded from recent national averages and are given only to show the range. Use the factors your reporting year and your auditor call for.
