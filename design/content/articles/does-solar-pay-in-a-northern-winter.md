Every November someone asks us whether solar makes sense this far north. It is a fair question to ask while looking at a grey roof at three in the afternoon. The answer lies in the year, not the month, and in how much of the summer's power the site uses itself.

## The year, month by month

This is what one kWp of panels gives on a flat roof, east–west at 10°, after losses:[^1]

| City | A year | December | June |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Gothenburg | 870 kWh | 8 kWh | 124 kWh |
| Malmö | 910 kWh | 10 kWh | 128 kWh |
| Rotterdam | 900 kWh | 14 kWh | 122 kWh |
| Leeds | 850 kWh | 13 kWh | 116 kWh |

In Gothenburg, the six months from April to September give 77% of the year. The four darkest, November to February, give about 8%. A summer that is duller than usual can cost as much as the whole dark season gives.

Cold helps a little. Panels turn light into power better when they are cold, by about 0.3% for each degree, so a clear March morning at −5 °C gives more for the same light than a hot afternoon in July.

## Snow

Snow on a low east–west roof slides off or melts within days. We do not clear it: the risk to people and to the membrane is worth more than the power. In southern Sweden and Denmark snow costs a typical system 1 to 3% of its year, nearly all of it in months that give little anyway. In Rotterdam and Leeds it costs almost nothing.

## What a kilowatt-hour is worth

The yield matters less than what each kilowatt-hour replaces. A kilowatt-hour you use on site saves the whole price of a bought one: the energy, the part of the network charge paid per kilowatt-hour, and the taxes. A kilowatt-hour you export earns the market price of that hour, less fees, and on a sunny day in May the market price at noon is often the lowest of the day, because every roof in the region is producing at once.

In 2025, the hours when solar produced were on average 15 to 30% cheaper than the year's average price in the four markets we work in.[^2] For power you use yourself, that hardly matters. For power you export, it matters a great deal.

## What decides the payback

1. **The share you use on site.** Size the system to the site's daytime use from April to September, not to the size of the roof. Above 80% use on site, the sums hold up well.
2. **What a bought kilowatt-hour costs you**, with every charge included.
3. **How long you will stay.** Panels last 30 years, and the inverters are replaced once, around year 12 to 15.
4. **The roof.** A membrane that has to be replaced under the array adds a cost that few forecasts include.

At the prices of the last three years, systems that use 80% or more of their output on site have paid back in 7 to 10 years in our Swedish and Danish projects, and in 6 to 9 years in the Netherlands and the United Kingdom, where bought power has cost more.

## And the winter?

Winter is when solar does least, and it is also when many industrial sites set their highest peaks: heating, lights on all day, and the morning start-up in the dark. If your problem is the peak on your network bill, solar will not solve it in January. That is [a battery's](/solutions/battery-storage/) work, and the two together cover more of the year than either does alone.

[^1]: Ampfield's yield model for east–west systems at 10°, run on long-term satellite measurements of sunlight for each city, after losses in cables, inverters, dirt and snow. The measured yields of our own systems in each region fall within 5% of it.

[^2]: Ampfield's analysis of hourly day-ahead prices in the price areas of southern Sweden, Denmark, the Netherlands and Great Britain for 2025, weighted by the output of a typical east–west system.
