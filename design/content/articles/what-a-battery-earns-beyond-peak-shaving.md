A battery that covers a factory's peaks works hard for a few hours a month. The rest of the time it stands full, waiting for the next one. Every customer who buys one asks what else it could do in those hours, and every sales pitch has an answer that adds up. This is what each job earned in 2025 in our four markets, and which ones look better in a brochure than on an invoice.

## One battery, six jobs

Take a factory in southern Sweden with a 2 MWh battery that can deliver 1 MW, bought for peak shaving at a price of around €725,000. Our model gives each job's earnings at 2025's prices:[^1]

| Job | A year | How certain |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Covering the peaks that set the network charge | €88,000 | Certain while the tariff charges for peaks |
| Storing the roof's midday surplus for the evening | €9,000 | Certain, in summer only |
| Moving power from cheap hours to dear ones | €14,000 | Varies with prices |
| Holding power ready for the grid | €15,000–35,000 | Varies a great deal |
| Keeping chosen loads running in a power cut | Not on an invoice | Worth what a lost batch costs |
| Avoiding a larger grid connection | Once, not yearly | Can be the largest of all |

Peak shaving alone pays the battery back in about eight years. With the solar and the price shifting, in six and a half. With the grid's money too, in five to six. The first line does most of the work, and the rest shortens the wait.

## Storing solar

A kilowatt-hour stored at noon and used at six in the evening earns the difference between what the site pays for power and what the grid pays for its surplus, less about a tenth lost in and out of the battery. It sounds like the obvious job for a battery beside a solar roof.

It earns less than it sounds, because we size roofs for use on site. A roof that uses 80% of its output in the building has a surplus mostly at weekends and on holidays, and a factory closed at the weekend has no evening to use it in. This job pays well only where the roof was built larger than the building's daytime use.

## Moving power between hours

On an hourly price, a battery can charge in the night's cheapest hours and discharge in the evening's dearest. In 2025 the gap between the two in southern Sweden was worth cycling on about 200 days. After losses, and after leaving the battery full for the morning peak, it earned this battery €14,000.

Bought for this alone, the battery would take fifty years to pay back. And on a supply contract with a fixed price, moving power between hours earns nothing at all.

## Holding power ready for the grid

The grid operator pays to have power held ready to steady the grid's frequency, whether it is used or not. A battery answers within a second, which makes it good at the job, and part of its capacity can be offered on the hours the site does not need it. Pilot offers our customers' batteries together, since a single site's is often smaller than the market's smallest bid.

This is the income that has moved most. In 2023, a megawatt held ready in the fastest of the Nordic reserves earned more than €100,000 a year. By 2025, after hundreds of megawatts of new batteries had joined the market, it earned a fraction of that.[^2] Prices in the Netherlands and Great Britain followed the same path, a year or two later. We show this income apart from your savings in every offer, and model it at the low end of the last two years.

## Backup and the connection

A battery with the switchgear for it keeps chosen loads running when the grid fails: a cold store, servers, a kiln mid-batch. No invoice shows that. It is worth what you would otherwise lose, and the charge held back for it is taken from the other jobs.

The sixth job can be worth more than all the others. When Kessel Packaging's plant in Dordrecht needed more power than its connection gave, the network operator offered a larger one in three years. A 5 MWh battery was running in five months and kept the plant inside the connection it had. That value comes once, and only where the larger connection was real, needed and slow.

## The same kilowatt-hour, once

The jobs compete for the same charge. Peak shaving wants the battery full on winter weekday mornings. The grid reserves want capacity set aside and left alone. Price shifting wants it empty at night and full at six. Ampfield Pilot plans the next day each afternoon and gives your own peaks first call. In practice that means peak shaving fills the battery's winter, and the reserves and the solar most of its summer.

## Where a battery earns little

- **A flat load.** A site that draws the same power all day has few peaks to cover.
- **A tariff without a peak charge.** Where the network charges only for energy, the largest line in the table is gone.
- **A fixed-price contract.** Moving power between hours earns nothing.
- **A roof that seldom exports.** There is no surplus to store.
- **A battery sized for grid income.** That income has fallen, and will fall further as more batteries join.
