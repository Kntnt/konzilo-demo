Most of the depots I am asked to electrify in England are not owned by the company whose vans park in them. The warehouse belongs to an investor, the lease runs for ten years with a break at five, and the power comes in through a connection in the landlord's name. The fleet manager wants chargers by the autumn, and the first thing to settle is whose they will be.

## Whose capacity is it?

A depot's grid connection belongs to the building, whoever holds the supply contract. When a tenant's chargers use it, they use something the landlord will want to offer the next tenant, or may already have promised to another unit on the same estate.

So begin with three figures, in writing: the capacity in the connection agreement, how much of it the building uses at night, and how much the chargers may take. Sixty vans need about 250 kW overnight when the chargers take turns, and much more if they do not. A landlord who knows that the chargers will never take more than an agreed figure, because software holds them to it, says yes more readily than one asked to trust a fleet's habits.

## Three ways to own the chargers

| | The tenant owns them | The landlord owns them | We own them, the tenant pays per kWh |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paid up front by | The tenant | The landlord | Nobody |
| The landlord's consent | Needed for the works | The landlord's own works | Needed, and both sign |
| Maintained by | The tenant, or us under a service agreement | The landlord, or us | Us |
| The tenant pays | The power, recharged by the landlord | A charge per kWh, or more rent | A price per kWh delivered |
| At the end of the lease | Removed, or sold to the landlord | They stay with the building | The agreement passes to the next tenant, or ends |
| Suits | A long lease and a fleet that will stay | A multi-let estate | A short lease, and no capital to spare |

A tenant with eight years left is usually best served by owning the chargers: they will be paid back in two to four years by the difference between electricity and diesel. With three years left, buying chargers it may have to remove is a poor use of capital, and paying per kilowatt-hour for 8 to 12 years only works if the landlord agrees that the next tenant will take the agreement over.

## Leave the cables in the ground

In a van depot, the groundwork, ducts and cables are about half the cost of the charging, and they last longer than any lease. However the chargers are owned, agree that the ducts and cables stay when the tenant goes. The next tenant gets a yard ready for chargers, the landlord gets a building that lets more easily, and the outgoing tenant is spared the cost of digging it all up to restore a car park nobody wants restored.

## Metering and billing

Most disputes I have seen between landlords and tenants over charging were about the bill, not the chargers.

- **Meter for billing, not for show.** The landlord bills from sub-meters of a class approved for billing, one per charger or per group of chargers, never from an estimate or a charger's own screen unless that is certified.
- **Bill by the half-hour.** If the landlord pays a different price at night, the tenant's chargers, which run at night, should be billed at night prices. A flat average makes the vans pay for the warehouse's afternoons.
- **Decide who pays for the peak.** Part of the landlord's bill is set by the building's highest demand, or by the capacity it has agreed with the network. If the chargers are held below an agreed limit, agree that they add nothing to that part, and write down what happens if they ever do.
- **One set of figures.** Ampfield Pilot gives the landlord the building's totals and the tenant its own vehicles, from the same meters at the same hour, so the two sides argue less about whose reading is right.
- **The emissions.** The power the vans use is the tenant's to report and not the landlord's as well. Agree which kilowatt-hours each reports, as [Lena Bergqvist explained](/insights/your-electric-fleet-in-your-emissions-report/) last month.

## Seven clauses for the lease or a side letter

1. **Capacity:** the kilowatts the chargers may draw, and at which hours.
2. **Consent:** for the chargers, the cable routes and the groundwork in the yard.
3. **Meters:** which meters bill, who reads them, and how the other side sees the readings.
4. **Price:** the power at the landlord's cost for each half-hour, plus a stated fee, or a fixed margin.
5. **The end of the lease:** chargers removed or left, at what value, and the ducts and cables left in either case.
6. **A break:** what happens to the chargers, and to a per-kWh agreement, if the tenant leaves at a break date.
7. **Insurance and maintenance:** who insures the chargers, who services them, and who is called when one fails at two in the morning.

None of these needs a new lease. On the depots we have built for tenants, a side letter of three or four pages has been enough, signed before the first trench was dug.

If you are a tenant planning chargers, bring your landlord's connection agreement and a year of the depot's meter data to the [site assessment](/go/site-assessment/). If you are a landlord whose tenants are asking, we can plan the whole estate's connection at once, so that the first tenant to ask does not take the capacity the others will need.
